Privacy policy

Aegis is a browser that doesn’t collect data from the people who use it. This policy explains, first briefly and then in detail, what is stored on your device, who the browser may connect to and what you can decide.

Effective October 17, 2026 · Version 1.0

In short

  • Aegis doesn’t collect your data. There are no Aegis servers: the browser doesn’t send us information about you or about how you use it. All we see are the overall figures provided by the Microsoft Store and GitHub (section 2).
  • There’s no telemetry. Aegis sends no usage statistics, no studies and no crash reports of its own. Windows may send Microsoft crash reports for any app, depending on its settings.
  • Nothing goes out before you decide. In our measurements with a new profile, Aegis doesn’t connect to anything on its own until you choose what to allow on the welcome page, “You decide what connects”.
  • Every connection can be turned off. The few connections Aegis makes on its own (updates, security and blocking lists, video codecs…) have their own switch. Those that depend on something you do, such as installing an add-on or letting a website know your location, are explained below one by one, with how to turn them off.
  • Your passwords are encrypted on your device. They stay in your profile, protected by a primary password that only you know, and they aren’t sent anywhere.
  • The websites you visit do see you. As with any browser, every website receives your IP address. Aegis reduces what they can learn about you, but it doesn’t make you anonymous: it isn’t Tor Browser.

1. Who is responsible

Aegis is developed and published by Secret Studios, the name under which an independent developer works. On the Microsoft Store it appears as “Aegis Browser”, published by Secret Studios.

For any question about this policy, you can write to mrsecret_official@protonmail.com.

2. Personal data we process

Through the browser, none. Aegis has no user accounts, no servers and no analytics, and it doesn’t send Secret Studios any information about you, your device or the websites you visit.

What we see through the distribution channels. Because of how Aegis is distributed, we receive the following. It doesn’t include your name or account, although a memory dump may contain fragments of what Aegis had open at the time:

  • Microsoft Store. Partner Center, Microsoft’s dashboard for Store publishers, shows us aggregated acquisition and usage statistics for the Store version. It also shows us the crash reports Windows sends to Microsoft (Windows Error Reporting) when Aegis crashes, depending on the diagnostic data level set in Windows. They may include stack traces and, on Windows Insider devices, memory dumps. You can adjust this in Windows Settings › Privacy & security › Diagnostics & feedback.
  • GitHub. It shows how many times each file of the published releases has been downloaded. Since every update check downloads a small file (update.xml), that number is also a rough count of update checks. It doesn’t show who downloads or from where.

When Aegis connects to a third-party service (for example, to find out whether there’s a new version), the connection goes straight from your device to that third party; we aren’t involved and don’t see its contents. Who they are, what they receive and how to avoid it is explained in section 5.

If you write to us. If you send us an email, we receive your address, your name if you include it and whatever you tell us. We use it only to reply to you and we don’t share it with anyone. Messages stay in the mailbox until they’re deleted; you can ask us to delete yours. Our email service is provided by Proton AG (Switzerland), which stores the messages on our behalf.

If you visit this website, see section 11.

3. What is stored on your device

Like any browser, Aegis stores on your device, in your profile, what it needs to work: your history, bookmarks, cookies and website data, the cache, the permissions you give each site and your settings. None of it is sent to Secret Studios or anyone else, and you can delete it whenever you want from the browser’s privacy settings.

  • Passwords. They’re stored encrypted in your profile. Before saving the first one, Aegis asks you to create a primary password, without which they can’t be decrypted. Nobody else has that key: if you forget it, it can’t be recovered.
  • Two-step verification codes you add: encrypted with the same key as your passwords.
  • Moving your passwords to another Aegis creates an encrypted file, wherever you choose, that only opens with a six-word code. Unless you untick the box, it also carries your two-step verification codes. It doesn’t go through any server: you carry the file yourself.
  • Tor (optional). If you download it, the program and its state stay in your profile’s local folder until you press “Delete”.
  • Where your profile is. In the GitHub version, in the %APPDATA%\Aegis and %LOCALAPPDATA%\Aegis folders. In the Microsoft Store version, Windows keeps new profile data in a folder of the app’s own and deletes it when you uninstall the app. If you used the GitHub version before, your profile in %APPDATA%\Aegis isn’t deleted.

4. The first time: you decide

With a new profile, Aegis doesn’t connect to anything on its own. The first window shows the page “You decide what connects”, with a switch for each group of connections. Until you press “Accept”, nothing goes out; if you press “Not now”, everything stays closed. You can come back to that page whenever you like from Settings › Privacy › Connections, and changes apply right away, without restarting.

What you do yourself doesn’t depend on that page: opening a website you type or click (see section 8), installing an add-on or letting a website know your location (see section 5).

We check this rule by measuring Aegis’s traffic with a new profile: in ten minutes without touching anything, it made no connection attempts. If we find a connection that doesn’t follow it, we treat it as a bug and fix it.

We recommend leaving at least updates and security lists turned on: without them, Aegis falls behind on security.

5. Connections Aegis may make

This is the complete list of connections Aegis makes without you browsing: those with a switch on the “You decide what connects” page and, at the end, those that depend on something you do. Each one receives, at a minimum, your IP address and the browser’s generic identifier (the “User-Agent”: the system and version, the same one millions of people use; it never says “Aegis”). The names in quotation marks are those of the switches on the “You decide what connects” page.

“Aegis updates”

GitHub version only. In the Microsoft Store version, updates are handled by the Store (section 7).

With whom
GitHub (github.com and its file download servers).
When
Only if you allow it. One check when you accept; after that, once a day while the browser is open and each time you open “About Aegis” or the settings. If there’s a new version, it’s downloaded from there. The “Check for updates” button always checks, because you ask for it.
What it receives
Your IP, the User-Agent and the type of system (64-bit Windows, and whether the processor is x64 or ARM64), without cookies and with a fixed language, the same in every copy of Aegis. When a new version is downloaded, GitHub also receives your browser’s language.
What for
To know whether there’s a new version and install it.
How to turn it off
With its switch. Without it, you’ll have to check for updates yourself.

“Security lists”

With whom
Mozilla, through its Remote Settings service (services.mozilla.com and its file servers).
When
Only if you allow it. When you accept and then once a day. With “Website notifications” on, also at startup and when the server announces a change.
What it receives
Your IP and the User-Agent, without cookies and with a fixed language. Only lists that are the same for everyone are requested.
What for
To know which website certificates have been revoked and which add-ons or graphics drivers are dangerous.
How to turn it off
With its switch. Bear in mind that, without these lists, to know whether a website’s certificate has been revoked, Aegis asks the authority that issued it every time you open a new website (section 8): turning them off means more of those checks, not fewer.

“Protection lists”

With whom
Mozilla (Remote Settings), for the tracking protection lists, based on Disconnect’s. And the authors of the filter lists used by Shields, Aegis’s blocker: uBlock Origin (GitHub servers and, if they fail, Cloudflare’s), EasyList (easylist.to and easylist-downloads.adblockplus.org, run by eyeo), AdGuard (filters.adtidy.org) and Brave (GitHub servers).
When
Only if you allow it. Mozilla’s, once a day. The filter lists: the browser looks once a day and requests only the ones that have expired (some daily and, by default, the rest every four days); and all of them when you press “Update now” in Settings › Shields.
What it receives
Your IP and the User-Agent, without cookies, without saying which website it comes from and with a fixed language. To avoid downloading what hasn’t changed, the browser sends each list’s server back the version tag that server gave it; in theory, that server could use it to recognize the same browser even if its IP changes.
What for
So that Shields recognize new ads and trackers.
How to turn it off
With its switch. Without it, Shields block using the copy of the lists that comes with Aegis, which gets out of date, and tracking protection doesn’t recognize trackers by name.

“Video codecs”

With whom
Mozilla (aus5.mozilla.org, which says which version to use), Cisco (OpenH264 download) and, only if you turn on protected video, Google (Widevine).
When
Only if you allow it: when you accept and then at most once a day, at startup. Widevine, only if a website asks for protected video (DRM) and you turn it on in its prompt.
What it receives
Your IP, the User-Agent and, in the request address, the Aegis version, its language, the channel and the Windows version; in the Microsoft Store version, also that it’s the Store’s Aegis. No cookies and no identifier for you or your device.
What for
Video calls with H.264 (OpenH264) and protected video, such as that of paid streaming services (Widevine).
How to turn it off
With its switch; protected video, also in Settings › General.

“Page translation”

With whom
Mozilla (Remote Settings).
When
Only if you allow it. The list of languages, once a day; a language’s model, only when you ask to translate a page or download that language.
What it receives
Your IP and the User-Agent, without cookies and with a fixed language. From the models requested, the server knows which languages you translate between. The text of the pages never leaves your device: translation happens on it.
What for
Translating websites without sending them to anyone.
How to turn it off
With its switch. Without it, the option to translate doesn’t appear.

“Website notifications”

Off by default.

With whom
Mozilla (push.services.mozilla.com).
When
Only if you turn it on: a connection kept open while the browser is open.
What it receives
Your IP, the User-Agent, your browser’s language, an identifier for this browser assigned by the server, and the subscriptions of the websites you allow to send you notifications.
What for
So those websites can notify you even when they aren’t open.
How to turn it off
With its switch.

Add-on updates

Only if you install an add-on. It has no switch on “You decide what connects”.

With whom
Mozilla (versioncheck-bg.addons.mozilla.org, its add-ons store service; versioncheck.addons.mozilla.org if you check by hand) or, if the add-on names its own update server (those distributed outside that store), that server, run by its author.
When
Once a day, as long as you have an add-on installed.
What it receives
Your IP, the User-Agent and, in the request address, the identifier and version of each installed add-on, the browser version, the operating system and the language. Your list of add-ons can make your request stand out from others. If it’s the add-on author’s server, it receives your IP, the User-Agent and whatever the address set by that author asks for.
What for
Installing new versions of your add-ons, which often fix security issues.
How to turn it off
In about:config, by setting extensions.update.enabled to false; your add-ons will stop being updated. The “Update Add-ons Automatically” option in the gear menu of the add-ons page (about:addons) only decides whether new versions install by themselves: the check still happens.

“Google Safe Browsing”: not available

Aegis doesn’t use Google’s service that warns about scam websites and dangerous downloads: this version has no key for it, so there’s no connection at all. It also means Aegis won’t warn you about those websites using Google’s lists.

What only happens if you ask for it

  • Checking for updates yourself: the same as “Aegis updates”, with GitHub.
  • Downloading Tor (Settings › Privacy › Tor): the program is downloaded from the Tor Project’s servers, without cookies; the Tor Project sees that your IP downloads Tor. After that, the private window with Tor connects to the Tor network only while it’s open. If you choose bridges, your IP is seen by their operators (obfs4) or, with Snowflake, by a content delivery network, public STUN servers and the volunteers who act as proxies.
  • Your location, only if a website asks for it and you allow it: it’s provided by the Windows location service (Microsoft’s), according to your Windows location settings. Aegis uses no network location service.
  • “Use the site’s logo”, on a New Tab shortcut: requests the page and its icon from that website, without cookies and without saying where the request comes from.
  • Searching: your search goes to the search engine you choose (DuckDuckGo by default), with your IP, as in any browser. Suggestions as you type are off by default; if you turn them on, what you type in the address bar goes to the search engine as you type it.

6. What Aegis doesn’t connect

  • Passwords, verification codes and passkeys. Everything happens on your device: the password manager, code generation and QR code reading don’t connect to anything. Passkeys are kept by Windows Hello; Aegis doesn’t sync them.
  • Importing data from other browsers. It reads the other browser’s files on your device, without connecting to anything.
  • Dark websites. It works inside the browser, with what the page has already loaded.
  • What Aegis turns off in the code it’s built from: telemetry and crash reports, studies and experiments, promotional messages, New Tab content and ads, add-on recommendations, Mozilla accounts and sync and their breach alerts, captive portal checks, man-in-the-middle checks on certificate error pages, country detection by IP, and link prefetching.

7. The Microsoft Store and Windows

On the Microsoft Store, Aegis appears as “Aegis Browser”, published by Secret Studios. It works the same as the GitHub version, with these differences:

  • Updates are downloaded by the Store, not by Aegis. Aegis’s own updater doesn’t connect. It’s the Windows Microsoft Store service that talks to Microsoft, as with any Store app.
  • Microsoft knows which account installed Aegis. Windows and the Store see, on their own, the installation and use of apps on the system. That depends on Microsoft, its privacy statement and your Windows privacy settings, not on Aegis. Microsoft shows us aggregated statistics and crash reports (section 2).
  • No attribution check. The code Aegis is built from asked the Store, at every startup, which campaign it had been installed from. Aegis doesn’t make that request.
  • No Windows App SDK. In this version and the GitHub one, Aegis doesn’t load those Windows components, which include Microsoft telemetry.
  • When you uninstall it, Windows deletes the profile data (bookmarks, passwords, settings) it kept in the app’s own folder. If you want to keep it, make a copy first or move it to another Aegis. If you used the GitHub version before, your profile in %APPDATA%\Aegis isn’t deleted.

In both versions, Windows makes its own connections. When you open the installer or the file downloaded from GitHub, SmartScreen and Windows Smart App Control ask Microsoft whether the file can be trusted. And if Aegis crashes, Windows may send Microsoft a crash report, depending on its diagnostic settings. That’s done by Windows, not by Aegis.

8. What the websites you visit see

When you browse, Aegis connects directly to each website you open. As with any browser, that website receives:

  • Your IP address and, with it, your approximate country and city.
  • The User-Agent, the same one millions of people use; it never says “Aegis”.
  • Your language and time zone (except at the highest level of fingerprinting protection), your screen resolution, your window size and whether you use the light or dark theme.
  • Whatever you send it: forms, sign-ins, searches. If you sign in to a service, that service knows who you are, as in any browser.

Aegis reduces the rest: it keeps each website’s cookies separate, blocks ads and trackers, limits fingerprinting and removes tracking parameters from links. It also sends every website the Global Privacy Control signal, which asks them not to sell or share your data and has legal weight in some countries.

Certificate checks. When you open a secure website (https) whose certificate isn’t covered by the “Security lists”, Aegis asks the authority that issued that certificate whether it’s still valid. That authority thus learns which website you’re opening, and the request is often unencrypted. With “Security lists” on, that request is rarely needed. It can be turned off entirely in about:config (security.OCSP.enabled set to 0), but we don’t recommend it: Aegis would stop checking whether a website’s certificate has been revoked.

What Aegis can’t prevent. Your internet provider, or whoever runs your network, can see which websites you connect to. Hiding some data is also a signal: very few people browse with this much protection, and a determined website can tell you apart. What each website does with what it receives depends on that website and its own privacy policy.

Aegis isn’t Tor Browser. The private window with Tor hides your IP from websites, but your internet provider sees that you use Tor, and Aegis is easier to tell apart than Tor Browser. If your safety depends on anonymity, use Tor Browser.

9. Children

Aegis doesn’t collect data from anyone, including children. It’s a general-purpose browser that gives unrestricted access to the internet and includes no content filters or parental controls. We recommend that children use it under adult supervision.

10. Your rights

  • With us. The browser sends us nothing, and the statistics and crash reports in section 2 don’t let us know whose they are: we hold no data about you to access, correct or delete, and have no way to identify you. If you’ve written to us, you can ask us for access to your correspondence, or ask us to correct or delete it, by writing to the same address.
  • On your device. What Aegis stores is yours: you can view and delete it from the browser’s settings, or by deleting your profile.
  • With third parties. What the services in section 5 and sections 7 and 8 receive is processed by them, each under its own privacy policy: GitHub, Mozilla, Cisco, Google, Microsoft, Cloudflare, AdGuard, EasyList, eyeo, the Tor Project, the search engine you choose, certificate authorities and the authors of the add-ons you install. The same goes for Proton, which holds our email. That’s where you can exercise your rights, and the simplest way to avoid it is to turn off the corresponding connection.
  • Complaints. If you believe your rights haven’t been respected, you can complain to the data protection authority in your country.

11. This website

This website uses no cookies, no analytics, no scripts and no third-party resources: even the typeface is served from it. It’s hosted on GitHub Pages, a GitHub service that, as GitHub explains, logs the IP address of visitors for security purposes. That log belongs to GitHub; we have no access to it. Links to other websites lead to sites with their own policies.
